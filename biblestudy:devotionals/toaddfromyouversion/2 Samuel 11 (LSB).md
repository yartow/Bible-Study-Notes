# 2 Samuel 11 (LSB)
> ### David and Bathsheba
> **2Sam. 11:1**   Now it happened in the spring, at the time when kings go out *to battle*, that David sent Joab and his servants with him and all Israel, and they destroyed the sons of Ammon and besieged Rabbah. But David stayed at Jerusalem.

I once read an analysis of where David started his sin of adultery. Before the actual act of adultery, other sins were already present in his life, such as in this verse. In the context of the previous chapter, Ammon was an enemy that was bound to cause trouble and take revenge for its losses in the previous battle, and perhaps to save face as well. One of the first sins of David was that he did not **go out to battle**, which he should have. Perhaps he thought that God had given him so many victories that he would have won this one as well. This is a wrong thought, of course, and he should have worked just as hard during this battle, even though the Ammonites were defeated in the last war against them. Another reason for joining the battle was because there were still casualties on the Israel side and with good leadership this would be minimized. 

> **2Sam. 11:2**   Now when evening came David arose from his bed and walked around on the roof of the king’s house, and from the roof he saw a woman bathing; and the woman was very beautiful in appearance. 

This is indicative of another bad habit of David. The fact that **David arose from his bed [...] when evening came** indicates that David had been sleeping throughout the day, while he should have been working. Of course he could have been tired of other work, it could have been a power nap and there are other reasons to explain this. 
This verse also shows the sin on Bathsheba's side for she bathed in plain sight, even though it was evening. If the king was able to see it from the roof, other men on their roofs would have been able to see this as well and even clearer, for the palace of David would have been further away from Bathsheba's house. 

> **3** So David sent and inquired about the woman. And one said, “Is this not Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite?” 

Without thinking David already had in mind that he wanted her and asked for information about her. His sin here was to not turn away when he saw her. As a king he could have forbidden her to bathe in plain sight. The Bible commands women to dress modestly and the king could judge on behalf of the Law, so he had ample reason and right to do so, though it would be uncommon. Another mistake—not actually a sin—here is that he was not accountable to anyone. David can be forgiven for his lack of knowledge on the topic of accountability—just like many other church leaders even in this day do!—but it was a grave mistake for him not to humble #questions/language/english/spelling verootmoedigen, niet vernederen, afflict?, himself to confess his sins to another believer (cf. James 5:16) and to allow others to have the right to reprimand him, despite being a king (cf. #todo/opzoeken/reference when your brother sins, rebuke him, and Revelation 3:19, loving and disciplining one another go hand in hand).  

> **4** Then David sent messengers and took her, and she came to him, and he lay with her; and when she had purified herself from her ==uncleanness==, she returned to her house.

David was not sexually satisfied. Though this is not considered a sin in itself, the sexual addiction that flows out of it, definitely is. Now one can bring a number of arguments against this, but one thing is for sure, he sinned against his all of his wives by committing adultery. His sinned every time he married a new wife, took a concubine or a random woman to lay with, which is an adulterous act towards all of the former wives. An argument to defend the statement that he had a sexual addiction is that the definition of an addiction is anything that becomes bigger than God in one's life. Clearly, David had a lust for sex. [[The wives of David|He had at least seven wives and ten concubines]] but still was not satisfied and even broke the Law to obtain Bathsheba. 
He was also drunk with power, as he showed here by deliberately transgressing the Law of God and taking a woman he likes. Not only that, he *knew* she was married and could have taken any other woman. David had seven wives and multiple concubines. 

Perhaps Bathsheba went along with the messengers because she did not know the reason why she was called for. But most definitely when it was clear that he wanted to **lay with her** she could and should have denied the king. All that was possible from that moment on was either that the king raped her or let her go. Given David's character and immediate insight into his sin (such as when Nathan rebukes him in 2 Samuel 12) the former would have been less likely. 
Another indication of Bathsheba's #questions/language/english/spelling medeplichtigheid, deelname aan de schuld, is that **she came to him**. In the Ancient Near Eastern context #tags/culture/ane this was also a euphemism for sexual intercourse according to *MacSBNnkjv*, but aside from that it is (according to me) also one that shows reciprocity, both sides were willing. 

The purification in the part **and when [...] uncleanness** could refer to her menstruation. Here it is translated as happening after she laid with him, as if the menstruation had started after the intercourse. This does not really make sense, however, because the *purification* after her menstruation would of course only happen after her menstruation had stopped, which would mean that she stayed—and needed to stay[^1]—in David's house for a few more days. Another possibility is that her menstruation started a few days earlier and happened to stop around the time of her intercourse, after which she purified herself. Though it is uncommon to have intercourse during the menstruation, it was not unlawful (see Leviticus 15:24), but then again, the two of them were in an unlawful act anyway. 
The KJV, ESV and NIV translate this part as a parenthesis or reference *to the past*, that is she had purified herself (of her menstruation) before she went to David, as an indication that her pregnancy was by David and not by her own husband.
An even better explanation is found in Leviticus 15:18 which explains that both the man and the woman are unclean after intercourse until the evening. So after her purification, which would be her bath, she went home. #biblestudy/discovery

> **5** And the woman became pregnant; and she sent and told David, and said, “I am pregnant.”

This happened most likely three weeks after the intercourse, for conception would have taken place on the fifteenth day of her menstrual cycle and on the 29th day she would have menstruated. She would have known a week after that that her menstruation had not come. 
It is not mentioned how often she had intercourse with David over what period of time, but from the fact that *she returned home* it appears that it was only once and that she left immediately after the purification which was until evening. 
David probably did not want the whole palace to know about her presence there. It is possible that for that reason she did not even stay overnight. Given the fact then that she became pregnant so easily, after only one event, it seems she was a very fertile woman, and if this was true, the fact that she was not pregnant yet by Uriah indicated that   they were only just married. Another possibility was that God had only now opened her womb, i.e. given her this fertility. If she was just married, she would have been about 17 years of age (for people married around age 16 in those days #todo/opzoeken ).  #biblestudy/discovery King David was about fifty years old by this time. #todo/opzoeken

In all these things, Uriah was punished for his loyalty to his commander and teammates, he delivered his own death warrant and died by fighting for the king who had sent him to his own death.  #questions/language/english/spelling Even though Uriah is not a foreshadow or type of Christ, we do see similarities in the injustice done to him. Uriah was not a Jew but become one. His wife was taken by the King and she willingly went along with him. Uriah had done nothing wrong in all of this, and it is only because of his death that God acted and punished the king. 

> **7** When Uriah came to him, David asked concerning the state of Joab and the state of the people and the state of the war. **8** Then David said to Uriah, “Go down to your house, and wash your feet.” And Uriah went out of the king’s house, and a present from the king went out after him. **9** But Uriah lay down at the door of the king’s house with all the servants of his lord, and did not go down to his house. **10** Then they told David, saying, “Uriah did not go down to his house.” And David said to Uriah, “Have you not come from a journey? Why did you not go down to your house?” **11** And Uriah said to David, “The ark and Israel and Judah are staying in booths, and my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camping in the open field. Shall I then go to my house to eat and to drink and to lie with my wife? By your life and the life of your soul, I will not do this thing.” **12** Then David said to Uriah, “Stay here today also, and tomorrow I will send you out.” So Uriah remained in Jerusalem that day and the next. **13** Now David called him, and he ate and drank before him, and he made him drunk; and in the evening he went out to lie on his bed with his lord’s servants, but he did not go down to his house.

In [[The three types of sin]] I have explained the difference between the three types of sin mentioned in the Bible. In all of the above it is clear that David was not only sinning, and not only deliberately sinning, but he was *continuing* in his *deliberate* sin, or in other words, *transgressing*. David thought of a plan and lied continuously throughout the plan. The whole night and one after that David had been deceiving Uriah by hiding the one fact that Uriah had the right to know of. David plotted by sending **a present** (probably food) after him, and getting him drunk—even though they were in battle at the moment and they should not be drinking—and he urged him to sleep with his wife, while it was known practice to David—of which he set a good example—to stay ceremonially clean during war (It is not mentioned in the Bible whether this is compulsory though) (see 1 Samuel 21:6, where he and his men were clean even in a non-religious battle).
After v. 11 nothing is said anymore about David attempts to convince Uriah to go home. Perhaps Uriah's answer too clearly showed his conviction of loyalty to his team, showing real character. Even this did not move David's heart a bit. David was blinded by his own goal. So much so that he plotted an even worse plan, described in the next verses, which perhaps was already plotted beforehand as a back-up plan. 

> **2Sam. 11:14**   Now it happened in the morning that David wrote a letter to Joab and sent *it* by the hand of Uriah. **15** And he had written in the letter, saying, “Place Uriah in the front line of the fiercest battle and withdraw from him, so that he may be struck down and die.” 

Now he had not only defiled his own hands and that of Bathsheba, and trying to let Uriah sin by sleeping with his wife during battle or perhaps letting him go against his conscience, and making him drunk (which is a sin in itself), but he made Joab commit a murder—even though he already had committed these earlier #todo/opzoeken/reference revenge for his brother and was an evil person himself. Perhaps he had no doubts killing off one of his own men. His fault of course is that he did not disobey the king's orders and obey God's instead. His evil character lead him to follow Adoniram #todo/opzoeken/reference  in the blink of an eye, when a better positioned was offered, namely as head of the army of the "new king" instead of the actual king.

> **16** So it was as Joab kept watch on the city, that he put Uriah at the place where he knew there *were* valiant men. **17** And the men of the city went out and fought against Joab, and some of the people among David’s servants fell; and Uriah the Hittite also died. **18** Then Joab sent and told David all the events of the war. **19** And he commanded the messenger, saying, “When you have finished telling all the events of the war to the king, **20** and if it happens that the king’s wrath rises and he says to you, ‘Why did you approach the city to fight? Did you not know that they would shoot from the wall? **21** ‘Who struck down Abimelech the son of Jerubbesheth? Did not a woman cast an upper millstone on him from the wall so he died at Thebez? Why did you approach the wall?’—then you shall say, ‘Your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also.’”

David's message to Joab was concise and did not instruct how Joab should exactly murder Uriah. The best circumstances for Uriah to die were in the situation where there were guaranteed victims, which would have been near the city walls, because there would not only be enemies before the soldiers, but also above, from the top of the walls. He knew that David as an experienced strategist #todo/opzoeken/reference somewhere is said "Your father David is an experienced fighter/strategist". would reprimand Joab for this mistake and for the deaths of many soldiers besides Uriah, but Joab chose to do this deliberately, because it was less obvious if Uriah fell in this way. He could not retreat all of a sudden and leave Uriah behind, that would have been too obvious.
So here, Joab subtly adds one reference to Uriah by which David will understand his choice of attack.

> **2Sam. 11:22**   So the messenger went and came and told David all that Joab had sent him *to tell*. **23** And the messenger said to David, “The men prevailed against us and came out against us in the field, but we pressed them as far as the entrance of the gate. **24** “And the archers shot at your servants from the wall; so some of the king’s servants died, and your servant Uriah the Hittite also died.” **25** Then David said to the messenger, “Thus you shall say to Joab, ‘Do not let this thing be evil in your sight, for the sword devours one as well as another; make your battle against the city stronger and tear it down’; and *so* ==strengthen== him.”

I think David tells Joab here to **strengthen** Uriah (or the thought of him) by destroying the city as a vengeance. This is the utmost form of hypocrisy, for it is David himself who sent him to his death. Agamemnon simply used the opportunity as a legitimate way to engage in war with Troy, possibly for its riches.  #stories/general/ideas/myth write this story in short form. 

> **2Sam. 11:26**   Then the wife of Uriah heard that Uriah her husband had died, so she lamented over her husband. **27** Then the *time of* mourning passed by, and David sent and gathered her to his house and she became his wife; then she bore him a son. But the thing that David had done was evil in the sight of Yahweh.

It is not known whether Bathsheba at this time knew about David's involvement in Uriah's death. Later on, however, David would swear to Bathsheba that her son would be king. Again, it is unknown whether David had told Bathsheba the actual reason why her first son died and possibly the appointment of Solomon as king was a consolation for that. Lastly, nothing has been written about her forgiveness concerning the murder of her former husband. It was a tough time for her. Though she had now become queen, she had lost her husband and her son, and had to share her current husband with at least seventeen other women and address her husband as a king, for whom she needed to bow and prostrate herself (see 1 Kings 1:15).
