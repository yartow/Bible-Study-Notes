# 2 Samuel 14 (LSB)
> ### The Woman of Tekoa
> **2Sam. 14:1**   Now Joab the son of Zeruiah knew that the king’s heart *was inclined* toward Absalom.

Even though David's **heart was inclined toward Absalom** he still refused to see him—unknowingly hurting Absalom even more and still being blind to his own failure in punishing and executing Amnon.

> **2** So Joab sent to Tekoa and brought a wise woman from there and said to her, “Please pretend to be a mourner, and put on mourning garments now, and do not anoint yourself with oil, but be like a woman who has been mourning for the dead many days; **3** then you shall go to the king and speak to him in this manner.” So Joab put the words in her mouth.
> 
> **2Sam. 14:4**   So the woman of Tekoa spoke to the king, and she fell on her face to the ground and prostrated herself and said, “Save, O king.” **5** And the king said to her, “What is your trouble?” And she answered, “Truly I am a widow; my husband has died. **6** “And your servant-woman had two sons, but the two of them struggled together in the field, and there was no one to deliver between them, so one struck the other and put him to death. **7** “And behold, the whole family has risen against your servant-woman, and they say, ‘Hand over the one who struck his brother, that we may put him to death for the life of his brother whom he killed, and destroy the heir also.’ Thus they will extinguish my coal which remains, so as to leave my husband neither name nor remnant on the face of the earth.”
> 
> **2Sam. 14:8**   Then the king said to the woman, “Go to your house, and I will give a command concerning you.” **9** And the woman of Tekoa said to the king, “O my lord, the king, the iniquity is on me and my father’s house, but the king and his throne are guiltless.” **10** So the king said, “Whoever speaks to you, bring him to me, and he will not touch you anymore.” **11** Then she said, “Please let the king remember Yahweh your God, *so that* the avenger of blood will not continue to bring about ruin, so that they would not destroy my son.” And he said, “As Yahweh lives, not one hair of your son shall fall to the ground.”
> 
> **2Sam. 14:12**   Then the woman said, “Please let your servant-woman speak a word to my lord the king.” And he said, “Speak.” **13** Then the woman said, “Why then have you thought up such a thing against the people of God? For in speaking this word the king is as one who is guilty, *in that* the king does not bring back his banished one.

This is a very courageous act of the woman in accusing the king of being guilty. She is not only wise, but righteous as well. The **such a thing** refers to the avenging of blood. 
The **avenger of blood** is one who is a relative to a problem who is killed, and is legally allowed to kill the person who killed his relative, unless the killer runs of to one of the safe haven (see notes on #todo/opzoeken/reference ... this did not apply for those who deliberately killed others). David provides an exception to the allowance of the avenger of blood, which would be those who have no sons left and cannot continue the family line if their last son were to be killed. 
The woman is implying that David made himself guilty by speaking this word—**that not one hair... shall fall to the ground**—for he himself banished his own son.

> **14** “For we will surely die and are like water spilled on the ground which cannot be gathered up again. Yet God does not take away life, but thinks up ways so that the banished one will not be cast out from him.

Perhaps this is an argument used to indicate that God cares so much about people that even in situations where there seems no possibility to be saved anymore, God **thinks up ways**, and so David could and should think of a way to let his son return despite the fact that he should be executed for killing a prince. The ultimate resolution David thought of was to let him return, but not let him see the king, as a house-arrest or semi-banishment (v. 24). However—in my view—the solution was simple for Absalom had done nothing wrong, because he acted in stead of David who had not acted as he should have (see notes on 2 Samuel 13:39) and this could also be seen as an application of the right to avenge blood for what had been done to his sister.
It could be—but that is mere speculation—that it were these actions—the two years where David had done nothing about Amnon and the three years of unrighteous banishment plus the unspecified period where Absalom was not allowed to see David—that caused Absalom's rebellion. Be that as may, this rebellion happened for a cause, namely so that Absalom's real heart was revealed, for if he were so wicked to sleep with his father's concubines (which is not only a sin against God (among many, sex before marriage), his father but also against the woman (rape) he should never become king in the first place. Also, this even showed Ahitophel's wickedness as well—through his treason to David and by advising Absalom to do the wicked things he had done—and to some extent the faithfulness of Joab and Abiathar, even though these would later commit treason by choosing the side of David's other son, Adonijah. #biblestudy/discovery/godshiddenplans  

> **15** “So now, the reason I have come to speak this word to my lord the king is that the people have made me afraid; so your servant-woman said, ‘Let me now speak to the king, perhaps the king will perform the word of his maidservant. 

The woman returns to her act, pretending that **the reason** she pleads with David is because **the people [who want to avenge the blood of one son] have made [her] afraid**. (*MacSBNnkjv* explains this analogy, perhaps I should summarize it, or criticize it. It could be that Joab simply used a wrong analogy. For instance, the woman had two sons and would be ruined if both were to die, not necessarily emotionally, but economically as well. In David's situation this was not even the case, for he had many other sons. #todo/opzoeken/reference how many?)

> **16** ‘For the king will listen and deliver his maidservant from the hand of the man who would destroy both me and my son from the inheritance of God.’ **17** “Then your servant-woman said, ‘Please let the word of my lord the king be a resting place, for as the angel of God, so is my lord the king to listen *with discernment* through the good and evil. And may Yahweh your God be with you.’”

The analogy of the widow and her two sons is not as clear as Nathan's analogy of the two farmers with their sheep (see 2 Samuel 12). Joab compares his banished son Absalom with the brother who killed his other brother. Yet in the story, the other brother had to be killed, while David had no intention of killing Absalom. Also, why does the woman refer to **the people of God** (v. 13) and what does it mean when she says "**the people have made me afraid**"?  #biblestudy/questions  

> **2Sam. 14:18**   Then the king answered and said to the woman, “Please do not hide anything from me that I am about to ask you.” And the woman said, “Let my lord the king please speak.” **19** So the king said, “Is the hand of Joab with you in all this?” And the woman answered and said, “As your soul lives, my lord the king, no one can turn to the right or to the left from anything that my lord the king has spoken. Indeed, it was your servant Joab who commanded me, and it was he who put all these words in the mouth of your servant-woman; **20** in order to change the appearance of things your servant Joab has done this thing. But my lord is wise, like the wisdom of the angel of God, to know all that is in the earth.”

> ### David Brings Back Absalom
> **2Sam. 14:21**   Then the king said to Joab, “Behold now, I have done this thing; go therefore, bring back the young man Absalom.”

This part, **I have done this thing**, is most likely literally translated from Hebrew, meaning something like "I have made a decision".

> **22** And Joab fell on his face to the ground, prostrated himself and blessed the king; then Joab said, “Today your servant knows that I have found favor in your sight, O my lord, the king, in that the king has performed the word of his servant.” 

Joab was not prostrating himself out of humility, but perhaps out of gratefulness—regardless of how much of it was genuine—that he tried to deceive David with the woman from Tekoa, and that David did not reprimand him for it. At least Nathan told him directly afterwards that what he, or God, had meant with that analogy. 

> **23** So Joab arose and went to Geshur and brought Absalom to Jerusalem. **24** However the king said, “Let him turn to his own house, and let him not see my face.” So Absalom turned to his own house and did not see the king’s face.



> **2Sam. 14:25**   Now in all Israel was no one as handsome as Absalom, so highly praised; from the sole of his foot to the top of his head there was no defect in him.

Both he and his sister Tamar were very **handsome**, as well as his daughter. This probably means that his mother, Maacah, like David, was also good-looking (cf. 1 Samuel 16:12).

> **26** When he shaved the hair of his head (and it was at the end of every year that he shaved *it*, for it was heavy on him so he shaved it), he weighed the hair of his head at 200 shekels by the king’s weight. **27** And to Absalom there were born three sons, and one daughter whose name was Tamar; she was a woman of beautiful appearance.
> 
> **2Sam. 14:28**   Now Absalom lived two full years in Jerusalem, and did not see the king’s face. **29** Then Absalom sent for Joab, to send him to the king, but he was not willing to come to him. So he sent again a second time, but he was not willing to come. **30** Therefore he said to his servants, “See, Joab’s portion *of land* is next to mine, and he has barley there; go and set it on fire.” So Absalom’s servants set the portion *of land* on fire.

There probably were other ways to speak to his father. If Absalom actually meant what he said in v. 32 he could have just visited his father in the court. Sure, the guards might have restricted him from entering or in the worst case he would have been killed, as was the custom in those days (#todo/opzoeken/reference Esther), but perhaps all of that cost him too much effort to do and shamefulness of being rejected. He had rather do it the easy way and force Joab to **send him to the king**. 

> **31** Then Joab arose, came to Absalom at his house and said to him, “Why have your servants set my portion *of land* on fire?” **32** And Absalom answered Joab, “Behold, I sent for you, saying, ‘Come here, that I may send you to the king, to say, “Why have I come from Geshur? It would be better for me still to be there.”’ So now, let me see the king’s face, and if there is iniquity in me, let him put me to death.”

Absalom showed traits of narcissism #questions/language/english/spelling or perhaps better diagnosis, for he did not get what he wanted and simply set someone's land on fire. 

> **33** So Joab came to the king and told him, and he called for Absalom. Thus he came to the king and prostrated himself on his face to the ground before the king, and the king kissed Absalom.

This gesture by Absalom was merely a disingenuous show of respect.
