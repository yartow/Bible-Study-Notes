> ### LABORERS IN THE VINEYARD
> 
> **Matt. 20:1**   “For the kingdom of heaven is like a landowner who went out early in the morning to hire laborers for his vineyard. 

Even though a parable should not be allegorized and only the main message only should be extracted, it is obvious what Jesus means with certain elements of the story. However, in this parable many *think* the meaning of the vineyard and the workers are obvious, but it is not. The **vineyard** is often seen as the place where unbelievers are, which only need to be "harvested" #todo/opzoeken/reference because the harvest is great, but there are few workers. This would indicate that the **laborers** are the Christians, who need to harvest the unbelievers. However, the workers' attitude of **grumbling** (v. 11) does not match that of Christians, especially not because of a future reward in Heaven for the hard work of gathering unbelievers.  unless it is there truly are Christian who grumble because their reward in Heaven is the same as that of others who came to Christ much later in life. However, the Bible also indicates in many places that there is difference in reward based on merit. So an equal reward regardless of merit would contradict that. The last argument against this interpretation is that Jesus says that the laborers agreed to their pay. Assuming these laborers are Christian, this would mean they already are *in* the kingdom of God. They could have been idle, just like many Christians who are not actively involved in the spread of the Gospel, but the motivation of a Christian to start working is never the reward that one gets in Heaven. As a matter of fact, there is not a single Christian who knows what he is going to get, and the mere motivation for spreading the Gospel is to bring God's created beings back to Him in order to please the Father, that is the only reward the Christian aims for. #biblestudy/discovery

> **2** Now when he had agreed with the laborers for a denarius for the day, he sent them into his vineyard. **3** And he went out about the third hour and saw others standing idle in the marketplace; **4** and to those he said, ‘You also go into the vineyard, and whatever is right I will give you.’ And *so* they went. **5** Again he went out about the sixth and the ninth hour, and did the same thing. **6** And about the eleventh *hour* he went out and found others standing *around*; and he *said to them, ‘Why have you been standing here idle all day long?’ **7** They *said to him, ‘Because no one hired us.’ He *said to them, ‘You go into the vineyard too.’

I am not certain whether the word **idle** here has the meaning of people doing nothing, while they could do something, like people waiting for someone to hand them work, even though they could look for it actively.
The analogy is striking for unbelievers though. Unbelievers do not by themselves actively seek the truth of what it or of how to enter the kingdom of God. They do not even know what the Kingdom is or that is exists. The truth must be handed to them about the existence of the kingdom and how to enter it. This is as far as the analogy goes. Jesus is the one Who seeks workers. Those who accept, will receive their reward. It is not clear from this parable whether the grumpling ones are Christians who envy other Christians who came to Christ late in life. A true Christian would not think this way and rather pity them for not having met and worked for Jesus earlier in life. Every Christian wishes that he had come to know Jesus earlier. 
The parable is not make a simile of entering the kingdom, and working in the vineyard is not the same as working for the ministry nor is it being part of the Kingdom of God. It is merely a principle that Jesus points out that He is *sovereign* to give whatever He wants to give and to Whomever He wants to give to. That is the first principle we can learn from this parable. The second is that the reward one gets is unrelated to the effort of man, it is purely by grace, which by definition is not earned. The third principle is to show that there will always be people who believe that God is not righteous. Jesus refutes this claim by stating that everyone gets what he deserves and agrees to the reward before he starts working for the Lord.

> **Matt. 20:8**   “Now when evening came, the owner of the vineyard *said to his foreman, ‘Call the laborers and pay them their wages, beginning with the last *group* to the first.’ **9** And when those *hired* about the eleventh hour came, each one received a denarius. **10** And when those *hired* first came, they supposed that they would receive more; but each of them also received a denarius. **11** Now when they received it, they were grumbling at the landowner, **12** saying, ‘These last men have worked *only* one hour, and you have made them equal to us who have borne the burden of the day and the scorching heat.’ **13** But he answered and said to one of them, ‘Friend, I am doing you no wrong; did you not agree with me for a denarius? **14** Take what is yours and go, but I wish to give to this last man the same as to you. **15** Is it not lawful for me to do what I wish with what is my own? Or is your eye envious because I am generous?’ 

Another important question is whether this parable refers to the kingdom of God in Heaven or here on Earth. If the laborers are **envious** and thus sinful, this the kingdom referred to here is that of Earth, in which Christian can still sin—*if* these laborers are Christians, that is (which I do not think, but see notes on v. 1). 

> **16** So the last shall be first, and the first last.”

This statement is quintessential of the Kingdom of God, and most Christians use it to refer to the serving character of the children of God (see v. 26-27). Within this parable, however, the topic of serving is not mentioned. As a matter of fact, there are three possible interpretations of this statement: (1) those who are last in this world, that is those who have given up everything will have most in the Kingdom (cf. Matthew 20:29); (2) the order in which one is called to serve is irrelevant for the reward. Those who served longer, will still receive the same reward as those who served only for a short while. (However, see also notes on v. 1); (3) In the Kingdom of God the rules and rewards are reversed compared to those of this world. The servants shall be leaders and vice versa (Matthew 20:26-27).

> **Matt. 20:24**   And hearing *this*, the ten became indignant with the two brothers. 

The fact these **ten became indignant** is because of jealousy. Jesus knew their hearts better than anyone else, and even more than their own. The reason for their jealousy also has to do with what Jesus said next, which is that the rulers of this world lord it over others. This is, in fact, what they were jealous of. Of course no one wants to be lorded over, anyone had rather be at the top than at the bottom, but they also liked being at the top, because of all the benefits that come with it. The power, wealth and status of most monarchs has decreased much in this time, compared to the kings of old, but kings both then and now still are far above the people. This is what the disciples longed for—to be served. But Jesus tells them that the leaders of his kingdom are not served, but rather serve. If this were known about leadership, then no one would want to be leaders anymore. The disciples, too, did not understand this when they first heard this, despite the fact that Jesus had repeatedly said this ( #todo/opzoeken/reference). They would understand through pain and suffering what it would mean to become actual leaders. The excelled in servanthood when they focused not on the power and wealth, but on their reward of meeting Jesus again and to be the first to do so. 
How soon do you want to meet Jesus? Would you walk or run towards Him? The disciples were asked the same question, but in their context of persecution the question was rephrased as this: do you walk or run towards punishment, suffering, mockery and death? #church/material/sermon

> ### SIGHT FOR THE BLIND
> 
> **Matt. 20:29**   And as they were leaving Jericho, a large crowd followed Him. **30** And behold, two blind men sitting by the road, hearing that Jesus *was passing by, cried out, saying, “Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!” **31** But the crowd sternly told them to be quiet, but they cried out all the more, saying, “Lord, Son of David, have mercy on us!” **32** And Jesus stopped and called them, and said, “What do you want Me to do for you?” **33** They *said to Him, “Lord, that our eyes be opened.” **34** And moved with compassion, Jesus touched their eyes; and immediately they regained their sight and followed Him.

It is striking to see that Jesus was *moved* with compassion, because the response the blind men gave was not unexpected. Can we say that just like when we watch a movie for the second time, we can still be moved by an actor, even though we too already know what is coming? Maybe so. On the one hand our
Jesus knows what is in their hearts, but unlike us—who can see a movie many times over and never understand what a character is truly thinking—Jesus not only knows what the blind men were thinking but also what they were feeling, and had been feeling all of their lives or at least ever since they were blind. 
In the same way Jesus feels all of our feelings. Even more so, on the cross He felt all the pain and suffering of every person who ever lived and would live, because true righteousness is according to the principle of an eye for an eye. This means that true justice is accomplished when the person who hurt the other feels what the hurt person felt. Since Jesus died for all those who hurt others, He also felt the retributive pain.
