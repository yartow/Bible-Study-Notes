> ### Judgments About Property Rights
> **1** “If a man steals an ox or a sheep and slaughters it or sells it, he shall pay five oxen for the ox and four sheep for the sheep.

God's sets the penalty at a 500% fine for expensive things and 400% for anything under that, unless the stolen item has been retrieved without harm (v. 4). The rules for donkeys, other animals or items are not mentioned, but the principle is that the more expensive something is, the bigger the fine is. This does not apply material goods, however (cf. v. 13).

> **2** “If the thief is caught while breaking in and is struck so that he dies, there will be no bloodguiltiness on his account. **3** But if the sun has risen on him, there will be bloodguiltiness on his account. He shall surely make restitution; 

The logic behind this is not merely that the owner of the house could not see the thief in the dark, but also that the tension in the night is much greater causing the owner to be in a self-defense mode. Of course, it must be decided in court whether the owner was legitimately defending himself when he killed the thief, whether during daylight or at dark.
The main principle here, of course, is that the owner has a right to defend himself. This principle applies everywhere, but especially in one's own house. 

> if he owns nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft. 

The Biblical principle that what is stolen must be returned, also applies when the thief cannot repay it. He must then work with his body in order to repay it. This is why slavery in this sense is not a bad thing and much better than imprisonment. 

> **4** If what he stole is actually found alive in his hand, whether an ox or a donkey or a sheep, he shall pay double.

See notes on v. 1. I assume this includes the return of the stolen item, so e.g. the thief would have to return the sheep and return two sheep. The main principle here is that when what is stolen can be (safely) retrieved, the punishment is less. This applies only to animals and perhaps to materials as well. However, in the case of human beings, a kidnap always leads to the death penalty, regardless of the state of the kidnapped person. See notes on v. 7 for stolen *goods* instead of *animals*. 

> 5“If a man lets a field or vineyard be grazed bare and lets his animal loose so that it grazes in another man’s field, he shall make restitution from the best of his own field and the best of his own vineyard.

The Bible is extremely thorough in covering every kind of trespass so that no one is able to escape the Law, yet it does not do this through explicit laws but through general principles that cover every possible situation. For example, in this case a farmer has let an animal graze his **vineyard [...] bare**, and **his animal** is let **loose** and starts grazing on another field. He cannot use the excuse that the animal acts out on his natural behavior. First of all, the animal was let loose, and he could have put a fence, and if the area would have been too big for a fence, the first clause still covers and protects the second party, which is that the first farmer let his vineyard be grazed bare. The first farmer has a responsibility to keep his field filled. There must be food there for the animal, otherwise it can be expected of an animals to go to other places to grace. And if he could have expected that, he should have either grown more food or start building a fence. Either way, the first farmer is responsible for the damage.

In the same way, in the situation where there is a fire, the man cannot claim that there were thorn bushes and that those spread the fire. The first responsible one is the one who set the fire, but the second responsible one is the one who did not remove the thorn bushes, or anything that could have spread the fire.

This automatically answers the philosophical question of whether a company for doing the right thing when something is not stated in the law of a country. 
**Example.** In university I had a course *Ethics, Methodology and Philosophy* where this question was discussed. Can we expect companies to do the right thing, or should the state just make better laws? From a Biblical standpoint this is not even a question. This is the whole reason why the Bible is not as explicit as some hoped it would be. By deliberately not covering all possible situations in life, it is clear that the Law should be applied according to the spirit of the Law and not to the letter of the law, for there is a general principle that is found through all of its commandments. #todo/nogaftemaken

In Western society prison has three purposes: (1) punishment (2) keeping society safe from this person, and (3) reform. > **7** “If a man gives his neighbor money or goods to keep for him and it is stolen from the man’s house, if the thief is caught, he shall pay double.

The state of the **goods [...] stolen** is irrelevant for the punishment. The payment is always **double** that of the value. If this were e.g. a car, this would mean that twice the value of the car would need to be demanded of the thief. It is not mentioned whether the stolen car itself is returned. This is more righteous than the current law in the Netherlands. 
**Example.** I was once arrested for possession of a stolen bike[^1] (which I had not stolen, but had merely borrowed from an acquaintance). There was a specific fine for these kind of crimes, perhaps dependent on whether it was the offender's first time or not, and the value of the item. However, when I was asked whether they could impound the bike I agreed, yet the fine stayed the same. The Biblical Law is the twice the value of the bike (if I had stolen it) should have been paid. This has much more advantages (for administration, determine value of bike, etc.), and let the thief keep the stolen bike. Or return it, but diminish the punishment. 

The severity of the punishment (rule of the height of the value) in v. 1 only applies when the original stolen item is retrieved, otherwise the punishment would be increased to three times, or four times for objects of higher value.

> **8** If the thief is not caught, then the owner of the house shall appear before the judges to determine whether he laid his hands on his neighbor’s property. **9** For every breach of trust, whether it is for ox, for donkey, for sheep, for clothing, or for any lost thing about which one says, ‘This is it,’ the case of both parties shall come before the judges; he whom the judges condemn shall pay double to his neighbor.

This law can be interpreted face value, but perhaps the deeper lesson here is that the one who agrees to **keep [...] money or goods [for his] neighbor** has to take into account that he needs to pay back double the value of that thing that is lost or stolen. Of course, this could have been an accident, but in reality the truth is unknown and situations like these can lead to arguments and consequently the loss of relationships. It is therefore wise to consider the consequences and even reject one's neighbor's request for help when one knows that he cannot bear the responsibilities. 

> **10** “If a man gives his neighbor a donkey, an ox, a sheep, or any animal to keep for him, and it dies or is injured or is driven away while no one is looking, **11** then an oath before Yahweh shall be made by the two of them that he has not laid hands on his neighbor’s property; and its owner shall accept it, and he shall not make restitution. **12** But if it is actually stolen from him, he shall make restitution to its owner. **13** Now if it is all torn to pieces, let him bring it as evidence; he shall not make restitution for what has been torn to pieces.

The same warnings as in v. 7ff are now applied to animals instead of material goods. In the case of material goods the punishment is always twice the value of the good, while in the case of animals this is not specified, on the one hand because the punishment depends on the value of the animal (cf. v. 1), and on the other hand perhaps because an animal could be worth more aside from its intrinsic or market value. I am not necessarily referring to emotional value, but to—for example—small farmers to whom for example a single cow would be their main "tool" for their income. The difference with Western Law is that in Jewish Law the principle where punishments are proportional to the value applies to animals and not to material goods, while in Western Law this is the other way around, for example in the case of larceny where distinction is made between theft and grand larceny.

> **14** “If a man borrows anything from his neighbor, and it is injured or dies while its owner is not with it, he shall make full restitution. **15** But if its owner is with it, he shall not make restitution; if it is hired, it came for its hire.
