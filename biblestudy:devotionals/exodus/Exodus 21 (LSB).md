> **Ex. 21:2**   “If you buy a Hebrew slave, he shall serve for six years; but on the seventh he shall go out as a free man without payment. 

Note that not the same conditions might apply here as in when a thief sells himself when he cannot pay back the property he stole (Exodus 22:3) for there the man is *sold*, while here one *buys* a slave. The assumption is that one voluntarily put himself up for slavery because of poverty. *Prager* has an comprehensive essay on slavery in the Old Testament, with as its conclusion that slavery is not advised, but rather it is *advised against* in the Bible. The Israelites were advised not to have slaves or become slaves, since they used to be slaves in Egypt (#todo/opzoeken/reference  Paul), which is also the reason why the Israelites were not allowed to go back to Egypt. However, given the fact that slavery was present among the heathens, God made provisions for those disobedient Israelites who decided to go along with this practice. It can be compared the laws concerning divorce. Divorce was not allowed in God's eyes, but because the Israelites would do this anyway, God made provisions in the Law for *how* one should divorce and under *what circumstances*. 

Note also that this law is only about buying **a Hebrew slave**. Thus the laws did not apply for pagan slaves. However, it was expected of every Israelite to treat all people humanely and with discretion with regards to any specific circumstance. One difference is the Hebrew slaves were held up to a maximum of six years and were fully redeemed in the Year of Jubilee (see Leviticus 25:44 and notes there). This does not contradict the rules in Leviticus where Hebrews are not be made into slaves, but only as servants (Leviticus 25:39–40). This is clear from the treatment the Hebrew slaves get, as explained both there and in this chapter.

> **3** “If he comes alone, he shall go out alone; if he is the husband of a wife, then his wife shall go out with him.

There were several ways in which the slave would serve his new master. He could have made a contract with his master-to-be for a certain amount of money which he would pay off through his work over a certain amount of time. It was also possible that this period would be shortened if his wife and children were to help along as well. This way the new slave would not have to be separated from his wife, or have to pay for their living expenses. However, it had to be clear that the husband, and not the wife, was the slave—and thus the master's property—under the set agreements.

> **4** “If his master gives him a wife, and she bears him sons or daughters, the wife and her children shall belong to her master, and he shall go out alone. 

Here is another distinction we have to make. First of all there is the Biblical rule, saying that the children must never be separated from the mother. Even though this is not explicitly stated in the Bible itself, it is common sense that God would not allow (young) children to be separated from their parents. On the other hand there is the rule of the culture, which Christians and Jews need to abide by up to a certain extent. Depending on the rules agreed to between the slave and his master, it was usually the case that the slave did not have the freedom to marry anyone while in service. His time was property of that of the master. However, if it pleased the master to give his slave a wife, for whatever reasons—this could be to harvest more slaves or to please the slave—this wife (almost certainly a slave as well) was the master's property and could not simply be given to the male slave. 

**Example.** If there any objections to this rule, we can look at the example of Abraham Lincoln, who did not expressly commanded in the law that slaves could be taken away from slave owners. If he had done that, he would have gone against his own rules described in the xth Amendment and set a precedent against it that private property could not be seized by the government. The first thing he had to do was to define slaves not as being property but as human beings who each obtained their rights from God. Only after that could he free the slaves.

> **Ex. 21:7**   “And if a man sells his daughter as a female slave, she is not to go free as the male slaves do. 

Even though this sounds cruel, this and also all other examples must be interpreted under the assumption that slave owners were good masters. Abuse of slaves was never allowed in God's Law. In this case the man **sells his daughter** because the family is in need of money. It would be like telling your son or daughter to work for a company or household for a number of years to help the family economically. This is still very common. Of course, in this age the common consensus is that that children should enjoy their childhood and education, but slaves were sometimes educated as well, some were even lawyers and doctors. But even if there was no education, assuming the life at the other household was good, being enslaved was not necessarily as bad a thing as is usually portrayed in movies.
*Prager* adds to this that in this context the word slave is to be interpreted as servant (he calls it *indentured servitude*), and only a girl's family was allowed to sell her as servant (and it can be assumed that the family had no bad intentions), and even then this was only allowed when the family could not sustain itself anymore. 

> **8** “If she is displeasing in the eyes of her master who designated her for himself, then he shall let her be redeemed. He does not have authority to sell her to a foreign people because of his treachery to her. 

If a woman was sold it could very well be that a man took the slave as his own wife. This is the reason the woman was **not to go free as the male slaves**, because of the matrimonial covenant. This could mean that the woman was merely bought for sexual purposes.

A man was never allowed to take **another woman** as his wife—this is not what this verse says, but it was generally known that this was wrong and did not please God[^1]—but in case where it *did* happen because of disobedience to God, at least he had to give her the same rights, including the physical intimacy (the **conjugal rights** spoken of in the previous verse) as the other wives.
