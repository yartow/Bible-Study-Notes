# Nehemiah 7 (LSB)
> ### Census of First Returned Exiles
> **Neh. 7:1** Now it happened when the wall was rebuilt and I had made the doors to stand, and the gatekeepers and the singers and the Levites were appointed, **2** that I commanded Hanani my brother and Hananiah the commander of the fortress, *to be* over Jerusalem, for he was a faithful man and feared God more than many. 

It is unclear whether this is the same Hananiah, son of Zerubbabel (1 Chronicles 3:19), who would have been at least 95 years old by this time, or the Hananiah from 3:8 or 3:30 or another one. 

#todo/nogaftemaken In any case, he was chosen for his faithfulness and fear of God, not for his capabilities. 

> **3** Then I said to them, “The gates of Jerusalem must not be opened until the sun is hot; and until they are *there* standing *guard*, they must shut and bolt the doors. Also have guards from the inhabitants of Jerusalem stand, each at his post, and each in front of his own house.” 

The gatekeepers probably only worked while the gates were open. It would be logical if less people were on duty in the night than during the day. Nehemiah changed the standard of opening the dates at a fixed time. All around the world it is was a common practice for merchants to prepare their merchandise in the morning, but now the gates were still closed in the morning and it would several hours before any customers from outside the city would come by. For the merchants who were waiting outside the city this must have been frustrating, because their working day was now shortened. Where they used to be able to work from, say, 6 AM, preparing by finding a spot, setting up their merchandise, etc. and start selling from 8 AM until 6 PM, now, they could only start *preparing* at around noon. 
Nehemiah wanted there to be bright daylight when the gates opened so that no intruders could secretly enter during the circumstances with lower visibility at dusk. He took every precaution possible. 
Also, next to the standard guards on the walls, he added civilian guards, **inhabitants [...] each at his posts**, which were most likely strategic points throughout the city. Lastly, even *near* (NIV) or ***in front of* [one's] own house** (civilian) guards were placed. 
It was also common practice all through history that men of all ages—and sometimes women as well—were trained so that in times of war they could be called up. Nehemiah is making use of the people's training even *before* a fight with the people of the land has broken out. 
Though this is not part of the original message of the book of Nehemiah, there are lessons in leadership that we can learn from this. #tags/leadership The first lesson is that we need to change the standard when needed. Just like Nehemiah changed the standard opening times of the gates, there are process that might need to change in order to protect what is most important. These changes will always have negative effects. People from both within the city as well as outside will be angry or frustrated. If you make changes as the leader of your family, people within your family as well as those outside might not agree with you. I might even say they *will* not agree with you in most cases. Suppose you have a daughter who wants to go out to certain venues and come home after a certain time which you do not agree on. It is very common that both she as well as her friends might not agree with you, or that the parents of your daughter's friends will think you are being too strict or even get a bad image of how Christians raise their children. The right way of dealing with critique is outside the scope of this snippet, but John Maxwell #tags/people/christian has a good book #tags/books on leadership in which he discusses several situations. 
The second lesson we can take away from Nehemiah's actions is that a fight always goes on *at least* three levels—the standard areas of attack (the walls), at strategic points, and at the deepest core (in front of your own house). This lesson is a very elaborate one and can be implemented in multiple ways. In a company, for example, when there is a big change of policy and many people are getting fired, there is leadership and protection needed on the walls, that is those from outside, e.g. negative publicity and media, on the inside, e.g. rumors of colleagues and in order to counter this transparent communication is needed, and at the core, which is the leader himself. The leader needs to be aware of his own needs, his physical needs, how much stress and negative emotions he can handle, etc. This is only one example of an implementation in a company. There are many possible implementations on a micro level, such as leadership within a board, or on a macro level, such as leadership over multiple departments or facilities. This shows the versatility of the lessons of leadership which we can extract from the life of Nehemiah. 
Another important real-life military implementation is that of the guard in Israel which has been ever since its modern "conception" in 1946. Military guards are present all throughout Israel on the same three levels, at the borders of course, as in every country, at the city gates, but also at places between provinces where cars pass, either to pay toll, and at the entries of kibbutz community areas. 

> **4** Now the city was large and spacious, but the people in it were few and the houses were not rebuilt.

Additionally, the people were spread throughout Judah, not throughout all of Israel, but still this was a huge area to gain control over, with so few trustworthy or experienced people that Nehemiah had to his disposal. The #todo/opzoeken exact aantal people who had come with Nehemiah (Nehemiah 7:66), Ezra (Ezra 2:64) and Zerubbabel were about 50.000, when including those mentioned in Ezra, which are not mentioned in Nehemiah and vice versa, but excluding those who had been born in the mean time. Those who chose to live in Jerusalem and not elsewhere in Judah, were still few considering the size of the city. The people were most likely spread throughout Jerusalem which explains why Nehemiah and Ezra's reforms effectively had zero effect. Ezra speaks against intermarriages with people of other religions almost a year after he arrived in Jerusalem (Ezra 10). It seems most logical that these who intermarried are not the people who came with him from Persia but those who arrived earlier, that is with  Zerubbabel. Ezra had only discovered this after a year because he probably had his hands full on taking of the city alone. 
Nehemiah too, in the last chapter—upon his second return from Persia (Nehemiah 13:6-7)—saw many of his reforms and rules disobeyed in the time he was away. Aside from the reason mentioned in #todo/opzoeken/reference see notes on ... this could also have been due to the lack of control in rural and countryside areas.

> ### The Assembly and Their Gifts
> **Neh. 7:66** The whole assembly together *was* 42,360,

The exact number in this passage and in Ezra 2:64ff show that these were not approximations. Those numbers who do not agree internally with other passages have deliberately been left out. According to *MacSBNnkjv* this is because of people from different tribes than that of Benjamin and Judah. 
Only the animals that labored were included in this calculation, just like the servants and singers, who were also laborers. Animals that were used as food, such as sheep (Nehemiah 5:18) were not recorded.
