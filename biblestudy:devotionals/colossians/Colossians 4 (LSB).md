> **3** praying at the same time for us as well, 

At all times Christians need to persist in prayer, because it is the source of everything. It is like oxygen, one needs it himself to, but others need it just as much. Suppose one is pumping air for himself, if the other does not pump, then the first one needs to pump twice as hard.
So the Christian needs to pray for himself in order that he has strength to keep walking in God's way, but he also needs to pray for the weaker Christian, who does not have the strength to follow Christ and give up himself. And aside from this, he also needs to pray for those who are strong, like Paul, but who are imprisoned and need extra strength in these dire circumstances. There are so many other reasons to pray, but one last example is that the Christian also needs to pray for the unbelievers, for they cannot pray for themselves.

> that God will open up to us a door for the word, 

Another thing that Christians need to pray for is that God opens doors for them to spread the gospel. As a matter of fact, all the things that Jesus mentioned in His example of how to pray—the *Our Father* prayer of *the Lord’s Prayer*—those are all valid. But aside from that, what is written here is not an addition to what Jesus already said, because Jesus said that God’s will may be done, and included in God’s will is the command to love our fellowmen, and included within those fellowmen are believers and unbelievers. So these are separate prayer items that we need to pray for. First of all we need to pray for our fellow Christians—and especially those who are under persecution, who need our help, who need support and strength, and who also need relief. So support and strength on the spiritual field, of course, is necessary within the prison of their mind, but it is also need in physical prisons. In Paul's case, there was *literally* a prison, but in other people's case, it could be someone like Adoniram Judson, who was in Myanmar (formerly Burma) who was also in a literal prison, but his imprisonment was also on the spiritual field. Judson had translated a great part of the Bible into Burmese and this translation had just been taken away from him. He really had no way of how to solve this problem. In other words, he needed to be relieved of this problem. But then there came relief, I would say, through the prayer of his wife, Ann Judson. She found it miraculously in an unexpected place, hidden inside a pillow, which was thrown away by the guard who had taken this from Adoniram. This is the kind of relief that we can pray for for our fellow Christians.

> so that we may speak the mystery of Christ, for which I have also been bound, 

The second thing we need to pray for, when praying for others, is for unbelievers. Christians who are *not* imprisoned need to pray for the Christians who *are* imprisoned so that they will have open doors—both figuratively as well as literally (for example to be released from prison)—to be able to freely **speak the mystery of Christ** and convert many more. Ironically, though, that which we pray for—this boldness to speak the Gospel—is the reason **for which [Christians are] bound**. 
The Satan thinks he can trap Christians in this way and stop them from furthering the Kingdom of God, but his plan fails on many points. First of all, he does not control the prison doors. If God wants to He can open any door of any prison and let out as many Christians as he wants to. He has shown this on multiple occassions, for example, God released Peter and John from jail (Acts 4), He released Peter from prison through his angel (Acts 12:5–12, note that "earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church"), and He released Paul and Silas from a Philippian jail through an earthquake (Acts 16:26). Even in more recent times, we know of Corrie ten Boom’s story of how she was released from a concentration camp by an "administrative error". 
Second of all, even when God decides to keep Christians in prison, he sends unbelievers *to prison*—which is an act of justice as well—so that these can hear the Gospel. There the Christian are not only more powerful witnesses *inside* prison—because they show their faith by being even willing to give up their freedom—but also they will have experienced so much in prison that will be even more powerful witnesses of God once (and if) they are outside! God is not only good, He is efficient as well, doing acts of justice to criminals, evangelizing to the unbeliever, spiritual growth for the Christian in prison, utilizing his experience outside of prison to spread the Gospel even more, but also to encourage Christians, and training Christians outside of prison to pray for and give to those inside. At the same time He taunts the Satan, and the Satan "without knowing it" (but of course he knows it) is helping out to further God’s kingdom with every action he does. The kingdom seems to be put backwards one step, but the consequential effect is that it goes forward two steps.

> **4** that I may make it manifest in the way I ought to speak.

There are so many things the Christian needs to pray for, and every one of those items has levels within them. So we have now come to the prayer request item that God’s will may be done, and within this item there is the subitem that we need to love our fellowmen, and within this there is the sub-subitem of loving Christians and the sub-sub-subitem to pray for the persecuted Christians (see notes at v. 3) and another one to pray that the Gospel is spread to unbelievers (see notes at v. 3c #todo referentie toevoegen per notitie, a,b ,c, etc.). Under this sub-sub-subitem there is another item and that is **the way [Christians] ought to speak**. This is covered in the next verse.

> **Col. 4:5**   Walk in wisdom toward outsiders, 

Even though Paul is often portrayed as a stern man, strict and righteous as a Pharisee—and this may have been true before his conversion—but most likely he was a gentle man, loved by many and more than likewise loving others with an unselfish, sacrificial love. He is most outright about the sins of people, as is written in his letters (cf. e.g. 1 Corinthians 5) and probably because of the content, a tone was implied by the editors of different Bible translations by adding exclamation marks. However, it is not known whether the other disciples were less strict than he was, for much less of their writings than Paul’s have been preserved in the New Testament, and the material they wrote about was not as close to personal admonishments as Paul had to in both of his letters to the Corinthians. 
Paul’s gentleness is actually confirmed by the disobedience of the recipients of his letters. It was exactly *because* he was so gentle that they did not fear him. Paul *did not want* to be strict (cf. 1 Corinthians 5:21b), he wanted to be gentle. That’s why he *begged* the Corinthians that they would obey Christ so that he did not have to be bold (2 Corinthians 10:1–2). 
**Example.** It is very understandable that one does not want to be bold all the time. This will create a distance in the relationship. As a teacher myself I need to be bold and strict, because I need to show an example of abiding by the rules, rather than bending these. This will, however, make me the *bad cop*, and especially students do not always understand the importance of obedience. However, there are times when it is wise not admonish a student. For example, if I have been away for a long time, and the first thing I do when I come back is admonishing students and handing out *demerits* (a form of warnings). 
I believe that in the same way Paul was not too strict to the Corinthians when he met them. He had only met them four times in his life, and that was quite a lot, given the busy schedule he had and limited opportunities to visit cities at random. His whole itinery was scheduled by God and he only went to—or left—places when God told him to. 

> redeeming the time. 

So the first aspect of wisdom in evangelizing is applied in the right *timing*. Paul knew what to say and when to say it, and there were times when from our perspective he should have told his message to the people when he saw them—instead of doing this through a letter—but Paul must have had wisdom from the Holy Spirit in his decisions and actions. 
This timing, however, has nothing to do with **redeeming the time**—which is mentioned in this part of the verse—which means that the Christian should make use of the time he is given. Not everyone has a long life, and spare moments are rare, for those who are busy (and also need to rest from their work). The moments we have left to evangelize, or those moments where we meet an unbeliever "by accident" we need to redeem and not go waste. 

> **6** Let your words always be with grace, 

The second aspect of wisdom in evangelizing is the *way in which the Christian speaks*, which should **always be with grace**. Here, grace does *not completely* refer to the grace in the sense that we receive that which we do not deserve—i.e. it has been given to us as a free gift (cf. Romans 6:23b, Ephesians 2:8–9)—but to the "attitude" with which grace is shown, which is quintessentially named *gracious* in English[^1]. Perhaps a good translation of this word would be *gentle*, for this encompasses the soft and kind attitude which most associate with Jesus' behavior, which is even depicted as such in the series #tags/movies/series/christian *The Chosen*. However, this would not fully cover what Paul means here. 
What did I mean by *not completely* above? The **grace** meant here in the first place refers to gracious, but in the second place it also refers to the very definition of *grace*, namely that things should be given to those who do not deserve it. Examples of these are compliments to the one to whom one speaks—well meant and sincerely, of course. Something like when one discusses evolution with another person, where the Christian then says, "I can hear you have spent many more hours on the topic than I have." Paul used this in his letters as well, where he complimented his readers for what they did well, even though some had horribly sinned, which he would address a few paragraphs later. #biblestudy/discovery

> seasoned with salt, so that you will know how you should answer each person.

Of course, everything here is in the context of **how [one] should answer each person**, so aside from the compliments one also needs to provide serious content, that is, the **salt**. The salt seasons a food, it makes it taste better. But we should not interpret this only as words that need to sound better, for otherwise the last part of the verse would not be interpreted correctly. The last part is **"so that you will know *how* you should answer"**: the *how* is known now, which is with grace, but we cannot infer from this that Paul only speaks of homiletics. Rather yet, it is more likely that he is speaking of *rhetoric*, for his goal is to declare the mystery of Christ (v. 3).

> **9** and with him Onesimus, *our* faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They will inform you about the whole situation here.

**Onesimus** had gone to Rome with Paul. He had met him in a very "coincidental" way, having fled from his master at Colossae, Philomon, who probably was not a Christian at the time yet. Little did he know that his master would be converted later by Paul (#todo/opzoeken/reference  ) and that he would ran into Paul himself later on. Perhaps he too was converted by Paul. Now, Onesimus was carrying the great responsibility to bring this epistle to the Colossians, and not only this one but also the letter of Philemon, which *also* would be part of the New Testament *and* to return to his master. He did not know that he—as a mere slave—was holding the original manuscripts of two letters in his hands that would end up in the Biblical canon, both mentioning him and one of which even *about* him! For archeologists in Biblical scholars it would be a dream to be able to look at *fragments* of these manuscripts, even more so to be able to *hold* them (I would reckon that no scholar has ever touched any manuscript without gloves), let alone be mentioned in it. 


Onesimus was but a slave and an assistant to the assistant of Paul, but the reward he received for his loyalty is great: All over the world these two letters are read and Christians know about him. The point I am making here is the same as that of the previous verse. Stay low and humble on this Earth, and the reward in Heaven shall be much greater. 
> **Col. 4:10**   Aristarchus, my fellow prisoner, sends you his greetings; and *also* Mark, the cousin of Barnabas (about whom you received instructions; if he comes to you, welcome him);

Paul was not the only Christian in prison, there were many more, and also many more who were tortured or killed on the extreme side of persecution, and those who were socially outcast or persecuted in another way on the less extreme side. Either way, Paul merely asks his readers to remember him (v. 18). He does not elaborate on his suffering and how the treatment is. When he did speak on those things, and of his misfortunes such as his shipwrecks, it was only because it was part of the argument he was making. His goal was always to give glory to God (cf. for example Ephesians 4:10–12). 
Rather than asking for help for himself, he only requested for things that would grow the Kingdom of God. Anything he would receive—food, visits, clothes or meals, if allowed—was in the readers' own initiative and came from the heart. This is the way in which the Christian *should* be able to count on one another—not by *asking* for help, but by being allowed to *expect* it from one's own brothers and sisters.

> **13** For I testify for him that he has a deep concern for you and for those who are in Laodicea and Hierapolis.

One who prays for his children to follow God, for example, will also follow God in his own life, and his love for his children is visible in daily life.

> **15** Greet the brothers who are in Laodicea

Oftentimes we tell people to "send our regards" to someone, as a way of closing a meeting. Paul here, however, sincerely wanted the Colossians to **[greet] the brothers**. He did not have the luxury to write every church a personalized letter and therefore required the church to pass forth his letters and messages. The Church as a whole cannot depend on one evangelist to spread the Gospel, or on one deacon to feed help out the poor, but rather each one must aid and assist in all tasks of the ministry for the kingdom of God, both inside and outside of the local congregation. 
This letter was sent by Paul was read publicly to the members of the congregation, and each had to take the message to heart and carry out the instructions individually.

> and also Nympha and the church that is in her house.

Nothing is known about **Nympha**, but she gets an honorable mention because of **the church [she hosts] in her house**. The fact that her name is mentioned—rather than that of a man—indicates that she most likely was a single woman or a widow. The rather would be more likely, because it would be difficult for a single woman to own a place, unless she had her own business. Hosting an event, especially on a weekly basis, is a tedious job. It requires strict discipline to wake up in time, clean one's house, prepare food, and clean up afterwards. Of course there is help from other brothers and sisters, who will bring food and help out with some jobs too, but it stays a big responsibility *for an indefinite amount of time*. It is unclear when the church is big enough to move to another location. 
**Example.** I have had the pleasure and honor myself to have hosted a church in my house for a few times, mostly during the COVID-19 period #tags/history/corona, because a local school did not allow us to use their place for church gatherings anymore. Opening my house was indeed a job where I was constantly busy making sure my guests were comfortable. For women this is even harder, because women usually have higher standards and are more empathetic by nature. 

> **16** And when this letter is read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and you, for your part read my letter *that is coming* from Laodicea. 

This is most efficient—to send two letters to different churches and tell them to meet each other and exchange the letters. Also, it is an encouragement for the churches to look up each other.  
A lot of churches refuse to work with each other because of mutual differences and in order to prevent their members from learning false things about the faith from the other church. However, in Paul's day there was no luxury of having hundreds of churches in every big city, such as is the case now in the Western world. There was only church per city, such as the church of Colossae, and these churches did not even have many members—especially not when (many of) their members were arrested all the time. In Laodicea there might have been two churches, but as Paul already mentioned, the second church was a mere house-church, filled with a handful of people. 
Churches *should* work together, regardless of how many there are now, because they still have the same enemy. First of all, this shows the world that despite theological differences Christians can still work together. Second of all, knowledge will be interchanged and the members will learn what is good through the Holy Spirit. Thirdly, the younger generations need to be surrounded with Christians so that they can build their network *within* the Christian community instead of being absorbed by the world.

> **17** And say to Archippus, "Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you may fulfill it."

We do not know much about **Archippus** other than that he is mentioned in Philemon 2, where Paul asks Philemon—who is in Colossae—to send his regards to Archippus. Most likely Archippus was one of the people within his house, perhaps even his child, which is why Paul sends his regards through the personal letter to Philemon instead of through this letter to the church of Colossae. 
Paul has only a short but extremely important message to Archippus, that is to **[take] heed to the ministry [and] fulfill it**. Of course this can be interpreted in multiple ways, one being that of encouragement and the other that of admonishing. So if Archippus was *not* taking heed at the moment, Paul was warning him about it. But it seems more likely that Paul was encouraging him to fulfill it. 
There are many things in life that withhold fulfilling the ministry that God gives to the believer. Most of the times this concerns the daily struggles in life. Paul tells us that the soldier (of Christ) is not to be entangled in civilian pursuits (2 Timothy 2:4). Some of these pursuits might be necessary, such as a job to provide for one's family, but the key word is not to be *entangled* in these. One's eye must always be on the goal (#todo/opzoeken/reference ).
