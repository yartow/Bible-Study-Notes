# Ezra 10 (LSB)
### Ezra Makes Israel Swear an Oath
**Ezra 10:1**   Now awhile Ezra was praying and making confession, weeping and prostrating himself bbefore the house of God, a very large assembly, men, women and children, gathered to him from Israel; for the people wept bitterly. **2** And Shecaniah the son of Jehiel, one of the sons of Elam, answered and said to Ezra, “aWe have been unfaithful to our God and have 1married foreign women from the peoples of the land; ==yet now== there is hope for Israel in spite of this. **3** “So now ==alet us== cut a covenant with our God to put away all the wives and 1btheir children, ==according to the counsel of 2my lord and of cthose who tremble at the commandment of our God; and let it be done daccording to the law.== 

*MacSBNnkjv* and Tim Mackie of the Bible Project have opposite interpretations of this act of Ezra, although these are not necessarily contradicting. MacArthur thinks that Ezra made the best choice possible at that time, for it was either the stoning of all people who had sinned, including even their children #todo/opzoeken or this solution. Mackie, however, protests this because this solution was from the people, and even more so, from men who had sinned themselves, and not with any consultation of God. So the question is, had Ezra consulted with God? The men had even required this, implying to accept anything even if it meant a punishment for them, explicitly stating that **it be done according to the law**. 
This is difficult to answer, for Ezra had been **praying and making confession** and more, and had been afflicted most of the day before that. Would his mind not have been cleared by all of this? Would the response of the men not be an answer from God? And if not, would he not be able to see that the men's proposal was an immoral one?
In my own experience I often see that a solution appears after a period of fasting, but I have never obtained feedback about whether that solution was actually the right or the best one. Most likely we will never know the answer. Mackie tries to get the answer by comparing the three stories of Zerubbabel, Ezra and Nehemiah, all of which end up in a strange and perhaps unexpected anti-climactic, where the people have not really changed despite the three leaders' efforts. In this way I do think it is possible to extract a conclusion about one story under the *presumption* that all stories have the same theme, namely that all solutions will not have an everlasting effect unless the heart of the people is renewed. 

**4** “Arise! For *this* matter is 1your responsibility, but we will be with you; a==be strong and act==.”

Whatever decision Ezra was to make, he would responsible as a leader. Of course the individual punishment is for the transgressors themselves, but as the leader of the people and the teacher of the Word, he had to enact the Law on behalf of God, and initiate course of action. He was responsible for teaching the Word and enacting justice. If this decision he made was wrong, and he incorrectly let these people live instead of stoning them, Ezra would bear the consequences of God's wrath, for a leader always bears a greater responsibility (cf. #todo/opzoeken/reference Paul's words on this issue). 

Note that it is Shecaniah who proposes this course of action, who probably had not committed this sin and had no personal benefit in this. Of course, if he had sinned it would mean that he would not be exiled from the people of Israel if his proposal was accepted, but he would still have to leave his wife and children. Also, he would be incriminating his own father and five paternal uncles (v.26, see also notes in *MacSBNnkjv*) so this seems like an unbiased motivation. 

Shecaniah told Ezra to **be strong and act** and he did so. We see no doubt in Ezra's actions in the following verses.

> **Ezra 10:5**   Then Ezra arose and amade the leading priests, the Levites and all Israel, swear*an oath* that they would do according to this word; so they swore*an oath*. **6** Then Ezra aarose from before the house of God and went into the chamber of Jehohanan the son of Eliashib. He went there, *but*bhe ==did not eat== bread nor drink water, for he was mourning over the unfaithfulness of the exiles. 

Jehohanan was a person of high status. (See Nehemiah 12:23 he is mentioned #todo/nogaftemaken ). Both Ezra and Jehohanan did not know that Jehohanan's father-in-law, Meshullam, would oppose against their decision, and Meshullam was a powerful person (cf. Nehemiah 6:18 where he is referred to as the one where Tobiah gets his stature from. Nehemiah 4:4, 8:4, 12:13 show him as one of the heads of fathers' households). Still, they continued their plans. In the same way, Meshullam opposed one or both of the assembly's proposals (mentioned in 10:3 and 10:14) despite Ezra backing both of these—although Meshullam would find out later, in v.16, that Ezra backed the second one as well—perhaps knowing that his own son-in-law backed one or both of these proposals as well. This also showed character on Meshullam's side—namely to stick with what his conscience told him and not go along with any other person's opinion. #church/material/sermon

> **7** And they made a proclamation throughout Judah and Jerusalem to all the exiles, that they should gather at Jerusalem, **8** and that whoever would not come within three days, according to the counsel of the prince and the elders, all his possessions should be devoted to destruction and he himself separated from the assembly of the exiles.

It is unknown who **Jehohanan** was, but he most likely was a Levite, for further references to his forefather **Eliashib** (see 2 Chronicles #todo/opzoeken/reference ) portray the latter as a singer, if indeed the same Elishib was meant. Further references to Jehohanan in Nehemiah, however, could be to be other people (e.g. Ezra 10:28, Nehemiah 12:13,42, but see notes there). This is also confirmed by the fact that Ezra sought the counsel of **those who tremble at the commandment of [...] God**, assuming that Ezra was seeking council here at Jehohanan's place. It could also have been that he did not seek council, but discussed with him the next steps to take, such as how they would make a **proclamation throughout Judah and Jerusalem to all the exiles**.

Perhaps the assembly genuinely regretted their actions, but it would not be long before the people sinned again. It would take a mere 12.5 years before Nehemiah appeared as governor after which the people were emboldended to abide by the Law even more, but after his first reign #questions/language/english/spelling better word? there was another governor who ruled for about 9 years #todo/opzoeken and during these nine years Israelites had married foreign women *again*! (See Nehemiah 13:23) It is not certain whether these are Jews who had been disobedient in Nehemiah's absence or that these were Jews who were sent away in this chapter, but lived separately from the group, for they had been put away from the community.

> **Ezra 10:16**   But the exiles did so. And 1Ezra the priest separated out men*who were* heads of fathers’*households* for *each of* their father’s households, all of them by name. So they 2convened on the first day of the tenth month to investigate the matter. **17** And they completed*investigating* all the men who had married foreign wives by the first day of the first month.

Note that the end of the first part out of three, in threefold story of Ezra-Nehemiah—which is one story—which is about the First Exile under Zerubbabel, ends with the celebration of Pesach on the 14th of the first month. In this second part too, about the Second Exile under Ezra, the process ended on the first day of the first month so that the people could celebrate the Pesach with a clean slate. 
The end of the third part, about the Third Exile under Nehemiah, also ends with Nehemiah cleaning the people from anything foreign, but does not note the date.

> **Ezra 10:44**   All these had taken up aforeign women*as wives*, and some of them had wives*by whom* they had children.

The fact that only some of them had children seems to indicate that this practice had only started recently. It could also have been that God closed the wombs of the women, but I will rule out this possibility for the following hypothesis.

There are only two groups of people in Israel right now: the group of the First Exile and that of the Second Exile, who had been here almost half a year (Ezra 10:17) from the date of departure. They departed from Persia on the first of the first month and arrived on the first of the fifth month (Ezra 7:8-9), which means that if the above transgressions applied to the second group, the men of this group married foreign women and made them pregnant and their children had not been born yet when the wives were sent away. The only other possibility I can think of is that it was the First Exile who married foreign women. But in that case I do not understand why it explicitly states that only **some of them had wives by whom they had children**, for the first exile was under the leadership of Zerubbabel and that went well until his death, which is, by my estimation, thirty years before Ezra's arrival. In those years many, if not all of these foreign women could have and most likely would have had children.
