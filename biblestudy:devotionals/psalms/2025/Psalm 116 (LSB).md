Mr. (Sam) Littel explained two days ago about how one can give back to Yahweh. The answer given is—first of all—*nothing*. We cannot give anything to Yahweh and everything we were to give would technically not be beneficial to Him.
**Example.** Compare this with a student who wants to help his supervisor with scoring the PACEs of other students. However, when scoring, the student with the ==green pen== makes mistakes, leading the student who did the PACE work to think that he did well and go on without correcting his mistakes. 
**Example.** Let me give you another example. Sometimes when I cook I ask Nathan to help me with certain things. He'll need explanation on how to do things, I'll need to show him and I'll need to correct him or clean up after him when he messes things up. In all, the cooking isn't done any faster with him. Rather yet, he slows me down and I would have been done faster if I hadn't asked him to help me. But the reason I ask him to help me is out of love, so I can teach him things and spend time with him and get to know him more, and so that he can get to know me more as well. 
In the same way, whenever we try to help God, we are not actually helping Him, but He is helping us in getting to know Him more and in becoming more like Him.  

So the first answer I gave to the question of what we can do to repay God for what He has given us—which is life, and for some, salvation as well—is *nothing*. If we repay God, do we still need to be thankful for him then? No, by definition nothing was *given* if the other person pays for it, right? So if I go to the Hoogvliet and buy a loaf of bread, do I need to be thankful to the baker for giving me this loaf of bread? No. I might say, "Thank you", but that's only because he passed it to me in a friendly way, but I wouldn't be *grateful* to him, because I paid for it myself. 
**Example.** Another example is from the book #tags/books/history by Thea Beckman #tags/people/authors, *Stad in de Storm*—and sorry, I'm going to spoil it a bit—there are two women, a mother and a daughter, and the mother is about to be hanged because people think she is a witch. Later on, even though the mother is not hanged and has been able to prove her innocence, the both of them still don't have any house to live in, but a good man takes them in, asking for nothing in return, not for the food, the heat, the water they use nor rent. As for everything that God has given us, we cannot pay back the life he has given us, or the oxygen we've breathed, but suppose we can, and suppose we have this money and could in some way give it back to God, are we then in a position that we do not have to be thankful to God anymore?

The real value of God and of a parent is not in the material value, but in the emotional value. The fact that you still live in a home with your parents is because your parent *forgave* you when did wrong things. If your parents hadn't forgiven you, you would have lived in a foster home right now. 
If God hadn't forgiven you and wasn't patient with you, you would've died ten years ago and ended up in hell. But God *has* forgiven you and gave you another chance, and He does this every day—everytime you sin, He does this again—and He has kept you alive until now. It is for all those second, third and fourth chances that he gives us every day that we should be grateful to Him. 

Now what we can do with our *hands* to repay God is *nothing*, but there is something we can do in our *minds* and that is *being thankful*. You may have received a letter or a card before, right? ~Who has received a poem at a Saint Nicholas celebration before?~ The poem itself may be good, or it may be cheesy, but the real value is in the fact that the person has spent time on thinking about who you are and what you like. 
So, if we cannot do anything for God, we can still be thankful, and how can we be thankful? By spending time on thinking about *what* we should be thankful for.
And not only about the material things we've received, but even more so about the immaterial things we've received from God—His love, His mercy and forgiveness, His righteousness and justice, and especially His patience with *us*.

> **16** 	O Yahweh, surely I am Your slave,
> 	I am Your slave, the son of Your maidservant,
> 	You have loosed my bonds.

It is not completely clear to me what this verse means. Most likely the author ( #todo/opzoeken David?) is pointing out his lowly position as a slave and a child of a maidservant. I am not certain what rules exactly applied concerning slavery #todo/opzoeken , but when a maidservant delivered, while she was in slavery, her children would also be slaves. Yet now God has **loosed** the **bonds** of David. David expresses his gratitude towards God by comparing this with that of a slave who has been set free. 
Galatians 4:30 has almost the exact line (quoted from the Old Testament) but with a different meaning, namely that the son of the maidservant would not inherit anything by default.
