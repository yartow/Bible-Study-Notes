#### Essay. What is the new commandment? 
Many editors and authors of study bibles and commentaries agree that John does not explicitly mention what the new commandment is. *MacSBNnkjv* for example says the new commandment is given in [[7C9ADF9D-C21F-4777-8333-4F9630F89217|2 John 5]], but there it only explains what the *old commandment* is, namely that we love one another—John does not explicitly say what the *new* commandment is. Another view is that the new commandment is merely an expansion of the old one, like in John 14:33 #todo/nogaftemaken add notes from ESV version. 
In my personal view the commandment is given *explicitly*. It may *seem* hidden, because the answer is not directly mentioned after the introduction here in v. 7, but a few verses later, in v. 15. Another affirmation of this is that this verse is the first imperative that appears since v. 7 (and commands (often) start with imperatives). The following reasons further affirm this interpretation. First of all, John often uses this technique of providing "answers" *after* a parenthesis[^1] #biblestudy/grammar, just like Paul does in his letters #todo/opzoeken/reference. John uses this for example in 1 John 1:1, where he speaks about what he has heard and seen, but answers *four verses later* what it is that he has seen. He also does this in 1 John 5:9 where he postpones the answer until v. 11. 
Second of all, it is clear that verses 9-11 and 12-14 are (or should be interpreted as) two separate paragraphs. The first paragraph elaborates on what is meant by walking in the Light or darkness and what this entails. The second paragraph is about the three (age) groups John addressed in his letter and why he wrote to each of these three groups.
The answer then of what the new commandment is, is given—after this parenthesis—in v. 15 which is **not [to] love the world nor the things in the world**. See also [[/Essay: The New Commandment|notes]] there.

#### Essay. The relevance of age in the Bible
The **little children** John addresses here are *spiritual* children, but the **children** (without *little*) he addresses in the next verse are actual children or at least people who are young. This does not mean that they are under eighteen, though, as is commonly thought in the West. Eighteen is but a random cut-off point for the age that represents maturity and therewith *consent*. 
Jewish culture had different definitions for the stages of life that humans go through. For example, at age twelth a form of moral adulthood started, ceremoniously celebrated with the *bar* and *bat mitzvah* (for boys and girls, respectively), even though the children were not named differently after they have had their bar or bat mitvahs and were still called *children*. In Western culture children after the age of twelve do get a change of name and are called *teenagers* until their eighteenth, After that, they are (officially) called *adults* and have the age of consent, which is the only distinction made in age aside from the age at which a person is allowed to drive, or drink (strong) alcoholic beverages[^3]. 
In Jewish culture professional adulthood started at age 30, the age at which we see for example Jeremiah starting his career as prophet, although he was trained to be a priest #todo/opzoeken/reference, the same age at which Jesus started His mission with a formal baptism by John the Baptist. 
Isaac too, though often pictured as a mere child in children's Bible's, must have been at least 16 years old #todo/opzoeken/reference when his father Abraham was going to sacrifice him. Some say he was older #todo/opzoeken but certainly not less. 
Western stories would have most certainly added a description of the age, such as "his teenage son", because ages are deemed important in our culture, but we need to realize that this is not the case in every culture. In Chinese culture, for example, it is quite irrelevant what age someone is. #tags/culture/chinese Of course it is noted or memorized in some way, but usually the Chinese remember their zodiac sign, which is one of twelve animals, one for each year of the twelve-year cycle, and the Chinese each know the order of these animals and whether one is older or younger than the other. So instead of asking, "How old are you?" they ask, "What is your zodiac sign?" and they instantly know the other person's age. For example, 2023 is the year of the Rabbit, and if an older man says he is born in the Year of the Rabbit, his age is a multiple of twelve, and his actual age can be easily inferred. 
For the same reason, I would suppose, ages are rarely mentioned in the Bible. Aside from 1,2 Kings and Chronicles, the Bible does not mention, for example, how old Mary and Joseph are. A few ages of certain people in specific situations are mentioned, such as Abraham's and Sarah's age, to indicate the impossibility of the latter's conception, and Jesus' age at the start of His ministry, which is not even noted exactly but only as "*about* 30 years of age" (Luke 3:23). 
Having noted all of this, from John's perspective, who may have been eighty years old when he wrote this, almost anyone would have been "young". If he actually had children, they could have been more than sixty years old and still be called "child".

#### Essay: The New Commandment
The new commandment (v.9) is complementary to Jesus' original commandment to love the Lord and love one's neighbor (which Jesus cited in Luke 10:27 from Deuteronomy 6:5, Leviticus 19:18, and which he made new, or better translated, *refreshed*, in John 13:34). This does not *seem* to *fully* complementary, though. For example, let us name the set of everything in this world and outside of it *the universe*, and everything that a Christian should do—which is to love God and his neighbor—be called *A*, which is part of the universe. Further let the remainder in this *universe* be called *B*. Let us suppose that when Jesus says "Love *A*" this *almost*, but not exactly, equals "Do *not* love *B*". I say *almost* because it is still possible a person could not love B and also not love A. However, in God's universe one either loves one or the other, there is no third option. One either loves God or Mammon (cf. Matthew 6:24). So in this case—which is true, because we live in God's universe—the new commandment *is* fully complementary to the old commandment.

This seems to be a hard commandment, and indeed it is. We live in this world, and we literally live *from* it—we breathe its air and we eat its food. Still, this does not mean that we have to *love* it. The prisoner also does not have to love the place he is in, but should only love that which is godly, which is the soul of the prisoners and guards. In the same way we should only love the soul of our neighbors and our Father in heaven—just like Jesus' commandment—and nothing else in this world.
There is no harmony possible between the love of the world and that of the Father. It is not even possible to let these to co-exist next to each other. One cannot have 10 percent of love for the world and 90 percent for the Father in him, but the Father *hates* whatever is of the world and the same goes the other way around. 
What is in the world are **the lust of the flesh and the lust of the eyes and the boastful pride of life,** and all of these cannot go together with God in *any* sense. What the flesh desires, is a corruption of the needs that God has put in man. Man has a natural and healthy desire for food, emotional care and excitement—under which fall entertainment and sex. All of these things man has corrupted. We eat unhealthy things because they stimulate our tastebuds and we eat too much of that; our ego wants to be stimulated, we want to be feel cared for, but we love it so much that we became self-centered. All of these examples are about stimuli and we want to be stimulated all the time and are addicted to it. 
These things cannot go together with God's commandment because the universe is not divided into *areas* that are of God and of the world, but rather it is divided in such a way that *everything is of God*, but *too much of anything* is not from God. So whether it is food or sex or entertainment, all of it is good if it is done for the glory of God—thus, being done in the right amount and with the right person and with the right intention—but when it is done outside of God's boundaries, it becomes part of the world. So as for food, one either eats the right amount of food or not, and thus either glorifies God or not, respectively, and there is no possibility to have it both ways.
