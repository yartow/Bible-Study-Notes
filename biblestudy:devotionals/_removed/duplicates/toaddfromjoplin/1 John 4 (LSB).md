> **9** By this the love of God was manifested in us, that God has sent His only begotten Son into the world so that we might live through Him. 

John does not speak in mathematical formulae when he explains to us what love is, but he gives examples—just like Jesus did with parables when He explained the Kingdom of God (the Kingdom of God is *like a...*)—and expects to understand from these examples the true substance. In these two verses he gives us two examples. Through these two examples we discover two characteristics of "love"—not just love, but *agape* (Gr: ἀγάπη) love, the one kind of love that is most difficult for humans to obtain (see also [[1 John 2 (ESV)/Do Not Love the World|notes on 1 John 2:15 (ESV)]]). The first characteristic is that love pertains to a sacrifice of great worth. **God has sent** something of incredibly great value, namely **His only begotten Son**. There is no specific threshold, but if anyone gives some leftover food to a beggar, which did not taste well to begin with, generally speaking all will agree that this is not love. The second characteristic is found in v.10 and is that love is unconditional: **not [...] we have loved God, but [...] He loved us [first]**. 
There is a third element, but it is not a characteristic of love. By this I mean that this third element allows the receiving end of love to *experience* this love. Gary Chapman #tags/people/authors has written about an elements like this in his well-known book *The Five Love Languages* #tags/books/love. In it he speaks of the *type of love* that is passed on as a kind of language. When a person expresses his love by *giving gifts* for example his gesture is not received in the same manner as one who has a preference for spending *quality time* with another person. The main principle behind this is that the receiving end *does not realize* the cost and value of the love it is given. Take for example a person who has knitted a sweater for someone. The cost is merely a few euros worth of wool, but the hours and precision and creativity put into it are worth far more than that. When one does not tell the other person about this, the receiving end will not appreciate the gift as much as the giver would have wanted to. 
Chapman's purpose is to teach his readers to learn the love language of one's beloved and adapt to it so that they will receive more love, or to obtain insight in the existence of these love languages and appreciate the love that is received more. 
The element that John describes here is slightly related to this. God loves us, acts it out and *lets us know* about this! In v.9 He **sent His only begotten Son into the world** and lets us know that is **so that we might live through Him**. In v.10 He **sent His son** and lets us know that it was **to be the propitiation for our sins**.
